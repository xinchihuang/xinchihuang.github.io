01

Learning-Based Mobile Robot Navigation in Human Environments

Faculty mentor: Yi Guo (ECE)

Project description

DescriptionThis project investigates non-communicating interaction between robots and pedestrians, and focuses on developing human-robot interaction (HRI) grounded in human-human interaction for common ground and mutual understanding. Existing model-based methods on pedestrian dynamics cannot fully capture the complexity of human navigation in crowds, and when used in the HRI study, may freeze the robot once the environment surpasses a certain level of complexity. The objective of the research is to design robot motion planner to navigate seamlessly in human-centered environments. Inspired by the remarkable success of machine learning in various areas, the project explores whether mobile robots can learn to navigate like a human in human environments. Learning based robot navigation algorithms will be developed and tested in robot simulators.

Expected Learning OutcomesStudents will learn to use robot simulator (such as ROS Gazebo, Nvidia Issac Sim) to simulate autonomous mobile robots navigating in human environments. Students will also experience machine learning methods applied to motion planning (such as reinforcement learning, imitation learning), and robot motion control (such as PID control, dynamic trajectory tracking).

02

Enhancing Operator-Drone Interactions via Vision-Language Foundation Models

Faculty mentor: Hamid Jafarnejad Sani (ME)

Project description

DescriptionThis project aims to develop and experimentally validate an AI-enabled mini-drone system that leverages vision-language foundation models (VLMs) to enable intuitive human-drone interaction through natural language instructions (e.g., text prompts). The primary objective is to investigate how foundation models can translate high-level user commands into actionable autonomous behaviors for aerial robots such as multi-rotor drones. Example tasks include locating and tracking objects of interest, interpreting scene context, and autonomously executing navigation objectives specified by a human operator. The undergraduate researchers will design and implement a framework that integrates vision-language models with visual perception, target tracking, and autonomous navigation capabilities. The developed algorithms will first be evaluated in simulation and subsequently deployed on a mini-drone platform in the Safe Autonomous Systems (SAS) Lab for experimental validation. The project will assess the effectiveness of VLM-guided autonomy through real-time object search, tracking, and navigation experiments.

Expected Learning OutcomesThrough this project, the students will gain hands-on experience in drone hardware, robot programming, computer vision, and autonomous navigation. They will develop skills in implementing and evaluating foundation models for robotic applications, integrating perception and control algorithms on physical and simulated multi-rotor drone platforms, conducting flight simulations and experiments, and technical communication in a collaborative research environment.

03

Supervision and Tasking of Large Deployments of Unmanned Surface Vehicles

Faculty mentor: Brendan Englot (ME)

Project description

DescriptionThis project will investigate how to present human supervisors with the necessary data and situational awareness to oversee the execution of a complex mission by a large team of unmanned surface vehicles (USVs). USVs contribute to the safety and security of critical waterways, and they are playing an increasingly important role as their onboard sensing, computing, and communications become more capable. Despite this, they are also faced with many challenges on the open ocean, which include regularly occurring environmental disturbances that can impede and degrade their performance. Humans will play an important supervisory role in the successful deployment of USVs in large quantities, at large spatial scales, for long-duration missions. This project will investigate how the data associated with a large USV deployment can be presented to human supervisors in a manner that permits efficient monitoring and high-performance mission execution. The objective of the research is to design and implement a mission management interface that will allow human supervisors to easily monitor the progress of an entire USV fleet (composed of 100 or more vehicles), the allocation of tasks to smaller teams of USVs, the cooperative execution of those tasks, and the performance of individual vehicles, when necessary. Comparative experiments will be performed that investigate the mission outcomes and cognitive load associated with different interface concepts. State-of-the art simulators of different levels of fidelity will be used to monitor fleet-level, team-level, and vehicle-level performance.

Expected Learning OutcomesStudents will become acquainted with state-of-the-art robotics simulation tools (including ROS2 and Gazebo), as well as the various layers and modules of a state-of-the-art hierarchical autonomy architecture, including mission management, task allocation, task execution, and vehicle planning, maneuvering and control. Students will also be exposed to both classic and state-of-the-art algorithms for implementing the various modules of the autonomy architecture.

04

Maximizing Ankle Exoskeleton Fit and Alignment via Machine-Learning Methods

Faculty mentor: Damiano Zanotto (ME)

Project description

DescriptionWearable robots, such as exoskeletons and powered orthoses, must transmit assistive forces to the body while minimizing excessive relative motion and localized pressure concentrations that may cause discomfort. This project will combine affordable wearable inertial sensors (Movella DOTs), machine-learning-assisted design pipelines, and open-source, camera-based motion capture (OpenCap) to: (i) estimate the orientations and locations of the anatomical ankle axes relative to the lower leg during locomotion using a heuristic optimization procedure informed by wearable inertial-sensor data and recently introduced by the mentor’s laboratory; (ii) develop an individualized, self-aligning mechanism for an ankle exoskeleton based on these estimates, following a machine-learning-powered design pipeline developed in the mentor’s laboratory; (iii) fabricate an unpowered prototype of the exoskeleton through additive manufacturing; and (iv) conduct a proof-of-concept validation using OpenCap to quantify indicators of fit quality, joint-axis alignment, and interface stability (e.g., the magnitude of relative motion between the leg and exoskeleton) during ambulatory tasks performed with the self-aligning mechanism locked and unlocked. This project builds on a study of an individualized, 2-DOF ankle exoskeleton that used optical motion-capture data and heuristic optimization techniques to develop a personalized 3D-printed orthotic design accommodating ankle plantarflexion/dorsiflexion and inversion/eversion during ambulatory activities while minimizing human–robot misalignment , led by an undergraduate student. The proposed research will extend this work by enabling wearable-sensor-based estimation of the user’s anatomical ankle axes, thereby improving the applicability and scalability of the methods.

Expected Learning OutcomesStudents will gain practical experience with wearable inertial sensors and associated data-processing pipelines, including coordinate-frame transformations and signal filtering; machine learning methods for mechanism design; and the biomechanical validation of wearable devices. They will learn to plan a small-scale human-factors experiment, assess measurement agreement and repeatability, and interpret wearable-sensor data from a safety-oriented perspective rather than treating the sensors merely as data sources.

05

Learning-Based Sensing and Feedback Control of a Soft Robotic Finger

Faculty mentor: Jacqueline Libby (ME)

Project description

DescriptionSoft robotic gloves can provide comfortable assistance because their actuators conform to the hand. However, pneumatic soft actuators are nonlinear, and their motion varies with pressure, external load, and repeated cycling. This makes it difficult to command a desired finger posture using pressure alone. This project will investigate whether flexible-sensor measurements and a lightweight learned model can provide accurate bend estimates for feedback control of an existing soft robotic finger. The objective is to develop and test a data-driven sensing-and-control pipeline for one soft robotic finger. Using an existing benchtop testbed, the student will apply pressure commands while recording chamber pressure, flexible-sensor signals, and reference bend angle. The student will compare a conventional calibration model with a lightweight machine-learning model on held-out motions. The better estimator will be integrated with a PID controller to track target bend angles or slow trajectories. Performance will be evaluated using estimation error, tracking error, response time, and repeatability. The project is limited to one existing actuator and benchtop tests; no full-glove fabrication or human-participant experiments are required.

Expected Learning OutcomesStudents will learn to operate a pneumatic soft-robotic testbed, synchronize sensor data, compare regression and machine-learning models, and separate training and test data. They will also implement basic feedback control, evaluate performance quantitatively, maintain reproducible code and experimental records, and communicate results through a poster and oral presentation.

06

Learning-Based Robotic Casualty Manipulation for Disaster Response

Faculty mentor: Long Wang (ME)

Project description

DescriptionWhen disasters such as earthquakes, structural collapses, industrial accidents, and fires strike, injured victims must be reached and moved out of danger within a narrow time window, yet the sites are often too hazardous or inaccessible for human responders. Unlike search-and-rescue robots that only locate victims or drag them as inert loads, this project studies robots that physically manipulate the human body with awareness of biomechanical safety, so that relocating an injured person does not inflict further harm. Building on the co-PI's high-fidelity simulation framework that couples robot–body contact physics with biomechanical joint-level reaction analysis , students will develop and evaluate learning-based methods for grasp-stability prediction and biomechanically safe manipulation. Using the simulation framework (Gazebo for interaction physics and OpenSim for musculoskeletal reactions) together with a benchtop tendon-driven end-effector on a 7-DOF arm and a rescue manikin, the student will train data-driven models that predict whether a grasp will remain stable and use reinforcement or imitation learning to plan manipulation trajectories that limit joint-level reaction forces. The project is conducted in active collaboration with Corvid Technologies, the co-PI's Small Business Innovation Research (SBIR) award collaborator, whose biomechanics team contributes high-fidelity human body models. All work is simulation- and manikin-based; no human-subject experiments are required.

Expected Learning OutcomesStudents will gain hands-on experience with robotic manipulation, grasp mechanics, and physical human-robot interaction; robot simulation using ROS/Gazebo and musculoskeletal modeling in OpenSim; and machine learning methods including supervised learning for grasp-stability prediction and reinforcement or imitation learning for manipulation planning. They will learn to design reproducible simulation and benchtop experiments, analyze contact forces and biomechanical reactions, and communicate results through a poster, oral presentation, and technical report.

07

Uncertainty Estimation for 3D Environment Perception

Faculty mentor: Philippos Mordohai (CS)

Project description

DescriptionThis project focuses on quantifying the uncertainty of a photorealistic 3D map of environment that is reconstructed progressively as more data are acquired by a camera. The goal is to create embodied instances of the theoretical work on uncertainty estimation. The undergraduate participant will implement progressive 3D reconstruction by extending a dense SLAM system based on Gaussian splatting, such as the one by Matsuki et al. , to mark highly uncertain regions in real time and guide further image acquisition to reduce uncertainty. Currently, uncertainty is measured by rendering novel views in virtual cameras and measuring their consistency, but we expect summer projects to closely track the latest research by the doctoral students. The objective in the initial years of the REU site will be to create a prototype operating as a handheld system. As this particular technology matures, along with the other projects supported by this effort, the goal will shift to integrating the perception system with the uncertainty predictor on an autonomous platform performing exploration.

Expected Learning OutcomesStudents will learn how current progressive 3D reconstruction using 3D Gaussian splatting works, and will extend state-of-the-art 3D reconstruction systems with uncertainty predictors gaining experience with estimation theory and auto-differentiation frameworks, for optimization, along the way. They also learn how to deploy the software, initially on laptop or phone cameras and eventually on robots, gaining experience in ROS.

08

Energy-Aware Persistent Monitoring with Mobile Robots

Faculty mentor: Shirantha Welikala (ECE)

Project description

DescriptionMobile robots used in public buildings, retail stores, campuses, care facilities, and environmental monitoring missions must repeatedly visit important locations within the mission space and perform critical operations (e.g., sensing/monitoring of the surroundings) while operating with limited battery energy. Many existing planners optimize utilization/monitoring performance through tuning the visitation schedule while treating energy as a fixed budget, which can cause a robot to miss critical locations, stop before completing its mission, or operate overly conservatively. This project will develop an embodied-AI framework in which a mobile robot learns how its physical actions affect its energy state and uses that learned model to adapt its decisions. The student will instrument a TurtleBot4 in ROS2/Gazebo and on the physical platform to collect battery state, velocity, motor or motion commands, travel time, dwell time, and mission-context data during repeated monitoring tasks. Physics-informed and data-driven methods, including regression and Sparse Identification of Nonlinear Dynamics (SINDy), will be used to identify compact energy models relating robot actions to state-of-charge depletion. The learned model will then be integrated into an event-driven receding-horizon planner that decides which location to visit, how fast to travel, how long to monitor, and when to recharge. The robot will balance human-specified priorities, such as maintaining low uncertainty at safety-critical locations, against battery health and mission endurance. The resulting closed perception-learning-action loop will allow the robot to learn from the physical consequences of its actions and revise future behavior. Performance will be evaluated using monitoring quality, energy consumption, operating time, and robustness to changes in terrain, payload, and battery condition.

Expected Learning OutcomesStudents will gain hands-on experience with TurtleBot4, ROS2, Nav2, and Gazebo; robotic data acquisition and visualization; Python and MATLAB; system identification and physics-informed machine learning; optimization and receding-horizon control; and simulation-to-hardware experimental validation. They will learn to formulate a research question, design reproducible experiments, evaluate model accuracy and mission-level tradeoffs, and communicate results through a technical report, poster, and demonstration. Depending on progress, students may also investigate online model adaptation or multi-robot coordination under shared charging constraints.

09

Trust-Aware Human–Robot Collaboration

Faculty mentor: Ting Liao (SE)

Project description

DescriptionAs robots increasingly operate in human-centered environments, their effectiveness depends not only on their technical performance but also on how people perceive, understand, and respond to their behavior. The objective of this research is to develop and evaluate human-centered embodied AI methods that enable robots to estimate users’ evolving states and adapt their behavior accordingly. The project will build on a trust-aware human–AI collaboration framework in which trust is treated as a dynamic and partially observable state. Students will investigate how behavioral, interaction, and potentially physiological signals can be used to infer changes in trust, cognitive load, engagement, or uncertainty during HRI. Students will design simulated or physical human–robot collaboration tasks in which a robot provides recommendations, feedback, navigation assistance, or task support. Students will explore eye-tracking, physiological sensing, or other multimodal measures, and develop computational models that estimate human states and support adaptive robot behavior. The proposed approaches will be implemented and evaluated through robot simulation platforms or laboratory-based HRI experiments.

Expected Learning OutcomesStudents will design, develop, and evaluate human–robot interaction scenarios. They will also gain experience with experimental design, human-subject research methods, behavioral data analysis, and computational modeling. Students may learn methods such as supervised machine learning, sequential decision-making, reinforcement learning, mixed-effects modeling, multimodal data processing, or adaptive control. In addition to technical skills, students will develop an understanding of key human-centered AI concepts, including trust calibration, explainability, uncertainty communication, cognitive load, reliance, and human oversight.

10

Tool-Use Agentic AI for Wearable-Sensor-Informed Robot-Assisted Health Monitoring

Faculty mentor: Yue Ning (CS)

Project description

DescriptionThis project investigates tool-use agentic AI for human-centered robotic health monitoring, focusing on large language model (LLM) agents that reason over streaming wearable sensor data (such as heart rate, motion, and other physiological signals) together with electronic health records (EHRs) by actively querying signal-processing tools, invoking causal discovery modules, and retrieving external medical knowledge rather than answering from static parametric memory alone. Existing black-box deep learning models for wearable-based health prediction often produce accurate but opaque outputs, offering little insight into why an alert was raised or how a robot assistant should respond. The objective of the research is to design and evaluate agentic frameworks in which LLM-based agents plan, select, and sequence external tools, such as wearable sensor stream processors and causal discovery algorithms, to produce interpretable reasoning that can inform a robot's monitoring and assistive behavior. Inspired by the success of agentic AI on complex, multi-step reasoning tasks, the project explores whether tool-augmented agents can construct auditable reasoning chains that link wearable sensor readings to actionable robot behaviors, such as alerting caregivers or adjusting a robot's proximity and assistance, and whether users can interactively query or correct an agent's intermediate reasoning steps. Evaluations will be conducted on real-world clinical data.

Expected Learning OutcomesStudents will learn to process and analyze streaming wearable sensor data (such as heart rate, accelerometer, and other physiological signals) and build LLM agent pipelines using tool-calling frameworks (such as LangChain, ReAct-style prompting, or function-calling APIs). Students will gain experience applying causal discovery methods to structured clinical and sensor data, work with retrieval-augmented generation for grounding agent outputs in external medical knowledge, and practice designing human-in-the-loop evaluation protocols that connect agentic reasoning to robot monitoring and assistive behavior in simulation.
